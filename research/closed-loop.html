Closed loop

An area, not a project. With Simon Little (UCSF) and Jeffrey Herron (UW)

Deep brain stimulation is tuned in a clinic room over an afternoon, for a disease that behaves differently at home over a day. The premise of this area is that the limiting factor is the measurement, not the model.

Why it is a category

A closed loop is a system that measures the thing it is also acting on. That is a harder object to study than either half alone, because the signal you are reading is partly a consequence of what you did last, and because the interesting behaviour only appears on timescales and in settings a laboratory visit cannot reach. The work here is about earning the right to close the loop at all: getting synchronised implanted, wearable and video recordings out of somebody’s living room, and then showing that behaviour predicts neural activity well enough that stimulation can be driven from it.

The approach

Participants implanted with bidirectional neurostimulators (Medtronic Summit RC+S) were recorded at home while performing a standardised motor battery, with local field potentials from the subthalamic nucleus and electrocorticography from precentral and postcentral gyri, alongside bilateral wrist accelerometry and video-based pose estimation. The data-collection platform was itself a publication — an at-home multimodal ecosystem — because getting those streams synchronised outside a clinic is most of the difficulty.

With behaviour recorded at that resolution, encoding models can predict neural spectral power from behavioural features. Which features matters: temporally rich predictors — the full acceleration spectrogram, task labels — do substantially better than coarse summaries like mean acceleration. That is a result about what you have to measure before movement-related neural variance is visible at all.

What came of it

Two things, in opposite directions. The characterisation study asks what stimulation does to the signal: in the participant with stronger baseline model performance, higher amplitudes improved prediction across the sensorimotor network by amplifying a movement-locked spectral component — beta suppression alongside gamma enhancement — which is what an “information lesion” account of DBS predicts.

The Nature Biomedical Engineering paper goes the other way and uses the decoded motor signal to drive therapy: an adaptive algorithm that raises stimulation during movement, with improvements in dominant-hand movement speed and in participants’ own reports against an inverted control, and a machine-learning pipeline that tunes its parameters remotely. It is not first-author work of mine; that page is a short explainer of someone else’s paper this work fed into.
