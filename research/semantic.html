Semantic maps of the brain

2020–2026, with Jack Gallant at UC Berkeley

Brain viewer The method Number, space and time Thesis

Encoding models built on word embeddings predict cortical responses well and explain them badly. This line of work is about closing that gap — not by predicting better, but by changing the basis the prediction is written in.

The problem

A voxelwise encoding model fit to story listening gives every voxel a weight for every feature of the words a person heard. If the features are the dimensions of a word embedding, the model predicts well, and the weights are close to meaningless. Embeddings carry more latent semantic factors than they have dimensions, so the factors sit in superposition: they are correlated directions in one shared space rather than separate axes. Different combinations of those directions produce identical predictions, which makes the weights formally non-identifiable. A voxel that looks tuned to number will look somewhat tuned to time as well, and there is no way to tell how much of that is cortex and how much is the geometry of the embedding.

The approach

The Sparse Concept Encoding Model replaces the dense embedding with an overcomplete basis learned by sparse dictionary learning: a thousand non-negative concept atoms, each a single coherent semantic direction, each interpretable by the words that load on it. The encoding model is fit on those instead. Prediction accuracy is unchanged — which is the point, since the claim is about interpretability rather than performance — and the weights can now be read atom by atom. The method page works through it.

What it makes measurable

Once each concept has its own axis, maps of different concepts can be laid over one another and the overlap means something. The case that motivated it is the theory of magnitude: the claim that number, space and time are measured by one shared system. Three atoms correspond to the three domains, and their cortical maps meet in the intraparietal sulcus and the inferior frontal gyrus, bilaterally. The number, space and time page tells that story from the theory forward; the CCN extension carries it to nineteen listeners and asks how far a given voxel is selective for one quantity rather than shared across all three.

The nineteen-listener maps are published as an interactive viewer: the group average and three individual subjects, with a guided tour and a click-anywhere inspector that shows a spot’s tuning and the words it responds to.
