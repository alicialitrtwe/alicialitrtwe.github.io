Parkinson’s, measured at home

2021–2026, with the Gallant lab at UC Berkeley, and Simon Little (UCSF) and Jeffrey Herron (UW)

The paper The thesis chapter Thesis

Deep brain stimulation is tuned in a clinic room over an afternoon, for a disease that behaves differently at home over a day. The premise of this work is that the limiting factor is the measurement, not the model.

The problem

Parkinson’s disease is increasingly understood as a disorder of network dynamics: motor symptoms arise from aberrant oscillatory activity across the basal ganglia and sensorimotor cortex rather than from a static lesion. Deep brain stimulation is an effective treatment for it and remains an open-loop one, delivered at a fixed amplitude and frequency chosen heuristically, while a patient’s therapeutic needs move over the course of a day. Closing that loop requires knowing what the neural signal does while someone actually lives — which is not something a clinic visit can show you.

The approach

Move the recording into the house. Participants implanted with bidirectional neurostimulators (Medtronic Summit RC+S) were recorded at home while performing a standardised motor battery, with local field potentials from the subthalamic nucleus and electrocorticography from precentral and postcentral gyri, alongside bilateral wrist accelerometry and video-based pose estimation. The data-collection platform itself was a publication — an at-home multimodal ecosystem — because getting synchronised implanted, wearable and video recordings out of someone’s living room is most of the difficulty.

With behaviour recorded at that resolution, encoding models can run in the other direction from the semantic work: predicting neural spectral power from behavioural features. That turns out to matter. Temporally rich predictors — the full acceleration spectrogram, task labels — do substantially better than coarse summaries like mean acceleration, which is a result about what you have to measure before movement-related neural variance is visible at all.

What came of it

Two things, in opposite directions. The thesis chapter asks what stimulation does to the signal: in the participant with stronger baseline model performance, higher amplitudes improved prediction across the sensorimotor network by amplifying a movement-locked spectral component — beta suppression alongside gamma enhancement — which is what an “information lesion” account of DBS would predict.

The Nature Biomedical Engineering paper goes the other way and uses the decoded motor signal to drive therapy: an adaptive algorithm that raises stimulation during movement, with improvements in dominant-hand movement speed and in participants’ own reports against an inverted control, and a machine-learning pipeline that tunes its parameters remotely. It is not first-author work of mine; that page is a short explainer of someone else’s paper that this project fed into.
